# ADR-0030: An org storefront is a scoped projection of the marketplace, on one domain

- **Date:** 2026-09-15
- **Status:** Accepted

## Context

A selling organisation is a real aggregate here — it owns products, offers, shipments, earnings and a portal — and a buyer can see none of it. Nothing on the storefront names who sold a thing, and an organisation has no address of its own to hand to its own customers. For a small shop that closed its shutters to join this marketplace, that is the wrong side of the trade: it gave up a shopfront and received a catalogue row.

[multi-vendor-marketplace](../specs/multi-vendor-marketplace/spec.md) ruled this out explicitly — *"Per-organisation branding, domains, or storefronts. One storefront, many vendors."* That was the right call while the open question was how to split a portal between three audiences. It stops being the right call once the product being sold to a seller is presence rather than distribution, so it is reversed here deliberately.

Giving an organisation a public face touches its identity, the product page, the catalogue query, the seller portal and the crawl rules at once, and the shape of that answer decides how much of the marketplace has to be duplicated. Getting it wrong duplicates the money path.

## Decision

1. **A storefront is a projection of the marketplace, never a second application.** Same catalogue, same effective-price function ([ADR-0018](0018-one-effective-price-function.md)), same basket, same checkout, same courier rates — read through one more filter and dressed in the organisation's own chrome. Nothing about a product changes because it is being shown on a storefront.
2. **It lives at a path on the platform's domain** — `/store/<handle>` — not on a subdomain and not on a per-organisation custom domain.
3. **`/store` is a distinct prefix from `/org`.** `/org` is the seller portal: gated in `src/middleware.ts`, disallowed in `src/app/robots.ts`. A storefront is anonymous and must be crawled, so it cannot share the prefix, and the crawl rules admit `/store` explicitly rather than by omission.
4. **The handle is generated at creation, and the organisation may change it thereafter.** Its public address is its own to choose, and a name an algorithm picked for it should not be permanent. Renaming the organisation does not move the handle; only an explicit handle change does.
   - **A retired handle is held for 180 days, then released.** While held it redirects to the organisation's current handle and cannot be issued to anyone else, so links still in circulation keep working and the organisation can take it back. The window is a configuration value, not a literal at the call site — it is a policy that will be tuned, and tuning it must not be a code change.
   - **After the window it returns to the pool.** It stops redirecting and another organisation may be issued it. Handles recirculate rather than accumulating forever against organisations that stopped using them. Between release and reissue the address answers **404** — not 410, because 410 promises the URL is gone for good and a released handle may well come back as somebody else's shop.
   - **Taking a handle back deletes the redirect that pointed away from it.** Reverting from `b` to `a` while `a → b` is still live would otherwise be a redirect loop. The rule is unconditional rather than a revert-only special case, so no future path can reintroduce it.
   - **A handle changes only through an explicit, authorised operation of its own** — never a field on a general organisation update, never settable by the caller at creation. [Invariant 4](../../CLAUDE.md)'s prohibition on mass assignment holds; what changes is that the field stops being server-owned. There is no cap on how often, deliberately: a rate limit is a constraint that can be added on top later without disturbing anything else here, and guessing its number now would be guessing.
5. **The marketplace URL stays canonical for a product.** A storefront links to `/product/<slug>`; it never re-serves the same goods under a second address.
6. **One basket, one payment.** A basket may hold goods from several organisations and still splits into per-org shipments and ledger entries exactly as it does today.
7. **Publicity is a property of the query, not the page.** Storefront reads filter on the organisation being active and publishable, and never select a commercial column — commission, discount ceiling, tax identifiers, ledger, settlements.
8. **Branding is a fixed set of validated fields with one visual axis, an accent chosen from the palette declared in `globals.css`.** Every other design axis stays as [ADR-0022](0022-design-decisions-go-through-tokens.md) sets it.
9. **A storefront exists from the moment its organisation does, and nothing gates it on the way in.** No verification step, no approval queue, no publish switch — creating an organisation creates its shop, empty or not. `Org.isVerified` stays unread. An organisation with no goods yet shows a page saying so rather than an error or a blank grid, and the address is shareable from day one so a seller can put it on a card before listing anything.
10. **The control is on the way out, not the way in: `Org.isActive` withdraws an organisation from public view entirely.** One switch, and its storefront *and every one of its products everywhere on the platform* stop being reachable — listings, search, category pages, the marketplace, not only `/store/<handle>`. This is the compliance mechanism and the only one; there is no separate storefront takedown. It is reversible, and it removes nothing: the organisation, its catalogue and its history all survive, hidden. Review is a manual pass by the platform owner over shops that are **already live** — nothing is gated on the way in, nobody waits for approval, and an approval system is deliberately not designed now: a queue nobody has time to work is worse than a manual read somebody actually does.

## Alternatives considered

- **A subdomain per organisation** (`shop.bhendibazaar.com`) — the Dokan and WCFM default, and the option a reasonable person picks, because it feels most like the seller's own site. Rejected for now on three counts: a subdomain is a separate site to a search engine, so every new shop starts from zero authority and the one argument that justifies the feature — *people searching your shop name will find it* — gets weaker rather than stronger; it requires wildcard DNS on GoDaddy plus a wildcard certificate and host-rewrite middleware, which is infrastructure a solo project has to operate forever; and it moves NextAuth session and cart cookies across an origin boundary, which is real work on the one path that must not break. Decision 2 leaves it reachable later as a redirect **to** the canonical path, so nothing here forecloses it.
- **A custom domain per organisation** — the strongest offer to a seller, and the eventual paid upgrade. Rejected as a starting point: per-domain verification, automated certificate provisioning and a host-to-org lookup on every request is a product of its own, and the support burden when a seller's DNS breaks lands on one person.
- **A storefront with its own cart and checkout** — cleaner "this is my shop" story. Rejected because it contradicts what is already built: an order spanning two organisations already splits into per-org shipments and ledger entries, and a per-store cart would need a second checkout path, a basket-swap interaction, and a re-examination of settlement — all to make the product worse for the buyer.
- **A seller-arrangeable page builder**, the Amazon Stores shape. Rejected on size: block schema, ordering, per-block editors, validation and preview is larger than the whole rest of the programme, and every block is a way to break a layout that [ADR-0022](0022-design-decisions-go-through-tokens.md) currently guarantees.
- **A permanently frozen handle**, matching the rule [Invariant 4](../../CLAUDE.md) sets for `Product.slug`. This was the decision here until review. Rejected because the two cases differ in who does the choosing: a product slug derives from a name the organisation picked deliberately, whereas a handle generated during a backfill is picked *by the platform* — and freezing a machine-chosen public address onto a business is a poor trade. Freezing also does not prevent churn so much as redirect it: an organisation that cannot change its handle creates a second organisation to get a different one, which is worse for everyone. Decision 4's holding period recovers most of what freezing was protecting, for a bounded time.
- **Doing nothing, and surfacing the seller's name on the product page only.** Genuinely cheaper, and it is the first subfeature regardless. Rejected as the whole answer because it gives a seller no address to promote, which is the thing being asked for.

## Consequences

- ✅ Nothing on the money path moves. Price resolution, stock, checkout, shipments and settlement are untouched, so the storefront cannot introduce a pricing or payment defect.
- ✅ One domain, one sitemap, one set of cookies, no DNS or certificate operations, and search authority that pools instead of scattering across dozens of weak new sites.
- ✅ A seller gets a link on the day they sign up, and the platform keeps a single canonical URL per product.
- ✅ Decision 7 makes leakage a query property rather than a template discipline, which is the same device that keeps a buyer's basket out of an org's order view.
- ⚠️ The storefront will read less like an independent site than a subdomain would, and some sellers will ask for one. The answer is a later upgrade, not a redesign — but it is an answer that has to be given.
- ✅ Decision 4 means generation does not have to be perfect. A poor transliteration or an awkward collision suffix is fixable by the owner, which is also what makes the backfill for existing organisations safe to run without asking anyone first.
- ✅ An organisation can undo a handle change it regrets, within the window, and nobody else can take the handle while it is held.
- ✅ Handles recirculate. Nothing accumulates addresses it no longer uses, and a shop that folds frees its name for the next one.
- ⚠️ **After 180 days an old link can reach a different shop.** This is the real cost of releasing handles, and it is worse than a dead link: a card printed in year one and followed in year two may arrive at a stranger's storefront, with nothing to tell the visitor that is what happened. The window is the only thing standing between a printed handle and a misdirected customer, which is the argument for keeping it generous.
- ⚠️ **180 days is still short of how long search takes to move.** A permanent redirect is how ranking transfers from an old address to a new one, and that is usually counted in quarters. Half a year covers most of it; a shop that renames will still lose some of whatever presence the old handle had.
- ⚠️ **No rate limit means no floor on churn.** An organisation can change handle as often as it likes, each change starting a fresh 180-day hold on the handle it left. A determined one can hold several addresses at once by rotating through them — bounded, since each expires, but not prevented.
- ⚠️ An organisation can shed a reputation by changing its handle. Nothing here prevents that, and the redirect from the old handle is what makes it visible rather than silent.
- ⚠️ Decision 5 means a storefront's own pages carry the organisation's search ranking while its products' rankings stay with the marketplace. A seller who expects their product pages to rank *as theirs* will not get that.
- ⚠️ Storefront links are exactly the URLs that get traffic spikes, and storefront listings run the same uncached, unpaginated catalogue read the category page does. This makes the caching item in [BACKLOG.md](../BACKLOG.md) load-bearing rather than merely open.
- ⚠️ Seller-supplied copy and links become public under the platform's domain. Validation is stated in the specs; who watches what sellers publish is not, and that gap is now real.
